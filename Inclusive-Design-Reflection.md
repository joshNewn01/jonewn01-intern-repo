# Working with Vulnerable Populations

## Research and Learn

### Who are considered vulnerable populations, and what challenges might they face in digital spaces?

 - Who?
    - Older adults
    - Individuals with disabilities
    - Low-income/remote communities
    - Indigenous individuals
    - Women/Gender-Diverse individuals
 - What challenges would they face?
    - Exclusion and Inaccessibility
        - Rigid, market-driven digital systems and poorly designed interfaces lock out users who cannot navigate complex menus or lack assistive tech support
    - Safety Risks/Exploitation
        - Higher exposure to online scams, data breaches, phishing and targeted harrassment
    - Economic/Social Isolation
        - Inability to access essential services
    - Addictive and Manipulative Design
        - Commercial algorithms prioritize user engagement over safety
        - Causes exposure to harmful content or addictive content/behaviors

### What ethical considerations are important when designing or working with neurodivergent individuals? (Hint: Avoid overwhelming UX, respect sensory needs, ensure clear communication.)

 - UX/UI Design
    - Avoid overwhelming interfaces
        - Minimalism is key
        - Predictable and clean layout
        - Avoid these as they can cause sensory overload:
            - Auto-playing videos
            - Flashing animations
            - Sudden pop-ups
            - Loud color palettes
    - Provide user control
        - Allow for users to customize their view/environment
            - Dark mode
            - Text size/readers
            - Font changes (for Dyslexia)
            - Pause animations
    - Provide/ensure logical navigation
        - Use consitent layouts
 - Communicative/Cognitive Needs
    - Use clear/literal language
        - Avoid ambiguity, idioms, sarcasm or anything that may muddy the meaning of the text
    - Offer multi-modal information
        - Present information in multiple formats - text, images, audio, etc.
    - Write upfront instructions
        - Give clear step-by-step guidance for tasks
        - Explain why certain information or actions are required
 - Sensory and Physical Environments
    - Respect sensory thresholds
        - For physical spaces, consider the impact of fluorescent lighting, loud background acousticsm, intense textures or smells, or any other factor that may cause sensory overload
    - Design quiet alternatives
        - Provide dedicated low-sensory spaces or 'digital quite modes' so users or individuals can engage without intense stimuli
 - Co-Design and Inclusion Ethics
    - Include neurodivergent voices
        - NEVER design for a community without including or involving them
        - Include neurodivergent individuals in the research, testing, and feedback phases (at the very least)
    - Avoid harmful stereotypes
        - Do not treat neurodivergence as a deficit to be 'fixed'
        - Frame designs around accommodation, empowerment, and universal accessibility

### How can you make interactions and content more accessible for people with ADHD or Autism? (e.g., simple language, predictable navigation, reducing cognitive load)

 - Content and Language
    - Use plain language
        - Avoid metaphors, idioms, vague instructions, or industry jargon
    - 'Chunk' information
        - Break the information into smaller, more digestible chunks as opposed to a wall of text
    - Descriptive labels
        - Ensure buttons, links, and any other interactables are clearly and specifically labeled
 - Layout and Navigation
    - Keep layouts consistent
    - Use progressive disclosure
        - Only show essential details upfront, add extra information when requested
    - Include structural aids
        - Use breadcrumb trails, search bars, and clear page titles so users are always aware of where they are
    - Support full keyboard control
 - Distractions and Sensory Control
    - Stop auto-playing media
    - Reduce overall motion and clutter
        - Remove pop-ups, chat widgets, or busy sidebars
    - Offer a 'focus mode'
        - Let users switch to a clean reading mode/hide non-essential elements
    - Allow customization
        - Let users adjust font size, line spacing, color contrast/palette, etc.
 - Task Flow and Time Limits
    - Remove time limits
        - No countdown clocks for checkouts
        - If timing is required, let users extend it
    - Have step-by-step instructions
    - Simplify sign-in
        - Offer alternative authentication methods that do not rely on short-term memory

### How can we support neurodivergent team members in a professional setting? (Hint: Be clear in communication, respect different working styles, and allow flexibility.)

 - Clear and Direct Communication
    - Ensure that all communication is literal and explicit
    - Provide written follow-ups
    - Offer flexible channels
        - Have multiple avenues of communication between team members
    - Share meeting agenda in advance
 - Respect for Different Working Styles
    - Focus on output over delivery
    - Embrace alternative social engagement
        - Recognize that deep focus or skipping casual small talk is a valid work style
    - Allow processing time
        - Give people time to think, do not cut them off in a conversation or rush their response
 - Flexibility and Supportive Elements
    - Offer schedule flexibility
        - Allow hybrid work, remote dats, or adjusted start and finish times to manage energy and fatigue
    - Build sensory-considerate spaces
        - Provide quite zones, noise-cancelling headphones, or dimmable lighting options
    - Use one-on-one check-ins

## Reflection

### How can you adjust your communication style to be more inclusive of neurodivergent users and teammates?

For me personally, I can start using far more direct phrasing. I personally like utilizing idioms, metahpors and similes to get my ideas across, but this can be confusing for neurodivergent (and non-neurodivergent) colleagues so I will refrain from utilizing such phrasing to ensure that communication is direct and comfortable.

### What are some common UX or communication pitfalls that might make Focus Bear less accessible or supportive?

Though I found very little in the time that I have been using Focus Bear (about a week and a half now) there are a few hypothetical pitfalls it could fall into:
 - Inconsistent Layout
    - Though I did not see this in any area of Focus Bear, should a new feature be added and the layout of accessing/utilizing the feature does not fit the already established menu/function layout then friction can be created
 - Unclear/Clustered Information
    - Written text is kept in a large blob, making it difficult to read
    - Sections of pages are unevenly spaced, leading to overall visual clutter
 - Lack of Consideration for Sensory Issues
    - Utilizing loud color palettes
    - Implementing overwhelming/overstimulating animations or color changes

### What is one practical change you can make in your work to better support vulnerable populations?

For an incredibly simple example, one change to my work that would better support vulnerable populations would be the implementation of language aids - as this would allow for those who do not communicate English to the same level as myself to understand what my work is trying to communicate.
